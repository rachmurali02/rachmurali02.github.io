The Rain Gods' Verdict : ICC World Test Championship Final

It was Southee’s 600th, Watling’s last, and New Zealand’s first all in the span of a three-day drenched game of invigorating cricket. As Simon Doul put it, the “Ultimate Test” story was akin to David and Goliath. It was the moment where I believed that a couple of underdogs from a heartbreaking 2019 could find solace as they triumphed over the alphas in World Cricket.

As an Indian girl, a strong supporter of Jacinda Ardern, and a passionate advocate of the Black Caps’ brand of honest cricket, I have always found myself split between the two sides. Maybe the multicultural cosmos of Singapore planted the idea that home is where you deem it to be, and not necessarily only where you are born into. Having never been to New Zealand, it's definitely the unspeakable spiritual connection that makes me cheer them on in the stands.

It was a week etched out by the rain gods. Spectators excitedly pooled in only to be left staring forlorn at the puddles of water filling in, soaking their day tickets. I set alarms for 5:30, 6:30, and 7:30 (AM mind you) only to be welcomed by Google’s banner “Match Delayed”, covers pulled in, and a bursting twitter feed that chastised the ICC for their lack of knowledge on when the rain gods would arrive.

On the second day, there was more than rain coming from the clouds. It was a sky-snagging lad with a metronomically precise action, and an immaculate consistency of deliveries that suffocated the best batsmen in the world. My initial introduction to Kyle Jamieson was against Pakistan where he wrapped up their rather lean batting breadth for scraps of runs salvaged through extras. He is tall, boldly expressive, but rather shy in the dressing room as Kohli puts it. Along with Conway, it seemed like they came onto the world stage fully formed. Like the teenage rom-com, “How to build a better boy”, if I wanted to similarly conceptualize a “How to build a better pacer”, I could just conjure up a ‘Jamieson’. He was the fast bowling all-rounder that India has been searching for since the golden boy Kapil Dev won us our first World Cup. We search for them in the Vijay Shankars, Stuart Binnys, and Harshal Patels, but we settle for the Jadejas, and Ashwins. It's almost like Kohli brought Jamieson into the RCB squad to inspire a generation of Indian fast bowling all-rounders to fight fire with fire.

India had its own top guns too. Rohit and Gill exuded the traditional Indian batter confidence as they showcased textbook pull shots, and cover drives to rattle the Black Caps from their bowling plans. They looked poised for a monstrous total. But Southee and Wagner were unabating, almost mirroring the rain that stopped play every once in a while. They guarded the floodgates with an influx of dot balls, slowly mounting up pressure, and squeezing out wickets. They summoned Kohli and Rahane. 132 ball 44, it was important. It telegraphed the relentlessness of an assiduous seam. Kohli’s innings was special. Even if it was a bowling attack. It was a display of his tenacity that battled with his urge to score quick runs. Jinks’ fortitude and Kohli’s victory over his inner conflict painted an assuring picture of silent dominance, a sound little softer than what we have seen of the Indian boys over the last decade. The day itself was a testament to the game. They were equally balanced. It was just a question of who wanted it more.

Beyond Pant, the Indian batting did not have much. We tumbled along quite easily to the short pitched bowling of Wagner, the inswingers of Boult, and fifth stump balls that we tried to drive. 217 was not a poor total. It was up to our quicks to make it look as menacing as possible.

We responded well. Ishant sent off the recently crowned double centurion, Conway.Even in a game that dampened spinners’ hopes, Ashwin had the innate ability to squeeze spin out of a stubborn Duke ball. Our conquests were on-song, but Williamson, as always, threw us off guard. He possesses a supernatural ability to foresee the risk-reward ratio in every meticulous shot he selects. He is powerful, but not in the flamboyant sense. He punches through covers, he nudges behind backward point, he gently pushes for a cover drive. It's graceful. It's patient. It's assertive. He steadied the ship against the cathartic fury of Shami’s extraordinary seam bowling that has historically suffered in the English conditions. Williamson’s presence was loud. It echoed “I am here to stay”. He stayed even when the Indians were playing 9 pins with his partners, knocking them down one by one.

However, India’s bowling spell was anticlimactic to their tail-enders. It made me reflect on why most teams’ tails wag more than ours. Perhaps, it's just another bone to the phalanx of “specialists vs generalists” debates that cloud the minds of leadership coaches time and again. But, barring our gritty well-rounded lower order in Australia, we have never found rhythm with them. I don't mean that we have to sequester the batting powerhouses between number 7 and 9. But, I would love to see less-talked about stars like Shardul Thakur and Washington Sundar introduced even when there are no physical injuries plaguing our side.

As we spot the differences between the indistinguishable vertebrae of these teams, maybe this was the yellow patch on their heat maps. As much as I would like to belabour our batting woes in the third innings, I think collapses are the convention overseas. It's the X-factors, the unexpected heroics that are needed to save the game. Think Ashton Agar against England as a number 11 debutant, think Ben Stokes in Headingley, think Kapil Dev against England. When there is no light at the end of the tunnel, it's that Deus ex machina that leaves us brimming with hope. It's probably time to introspect on what really makes an “A team”. Is it the specialist mantra that we at Mumbai Indians chant for the T20s where your role is contained, or is it an amalgamation of skill sets hidden in a Pandora’s box waiting to be unleashed just like the Herculean slingshots a seemingly benign David hurled to topple an ever so mighty Goliath.
